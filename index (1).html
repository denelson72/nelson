The Case

What This Is About

Debra Nelson, a former employee of Curtiss-Wright — a major U.S. defense contractor involved in shipbuilding — filed a whistleblower complaint in 2023 after experiencing unlawful discrimination. What followed was a two-year coordinated campaign of retaliation involving cyber intrusion, physical surveillance, evidence destruction, and the failure of every federal institution designed to protect whistleblowers.

This site exists because the courts, the FBI, the Department of Defense Inspector General and a sitting Congressman's office have all failed to act. The public record presented here is the last recourse. Sunlight is the best disinfectant.

The Central Unanswered Question

"How did Congressman Clyburn's office transmit a document referencing Ms. Nelson to the Department of Defense — before she had given that document to Congressman Clyburn's office?"

Broader Pattern

Defense Contractor Accountability

The misconduct documented in this case is not an isolated incident. It reflects a pattern of behavior by defense contractors who operate under the assumption that their government contracts make them immune from accountability — immune from discrimination laws, immune from whistleblower protections, and immune from judicial scrutiny.

The Core Problem

"If a defense contractor's shipbuilding contracts are critical to national security — then the integrity of the people executing those contracts matters. Discrimination, retaliation, and cover-up inside a defense contractor is not just a labor issue. It is a national security concern."

The Connection
⚠ Interference at a Veterans Legal Clinic — March 12, 2026

On March 12, 2026, Ms. Nelson drove from South Carolina to Tallahassee, Florida to attend a free Veterans Legal Collaborative clinic at American Legion Sauls-Bridges Post 13. Her sole purpose was to have an attorney briefly review her amended complaint for shotgun pleadings before a federal filing deadline the following day.

Ms. Nelson was paired with Cindy, the coordinator of the Veterans Legal Collaborative clinic. When Ms. Nelson showed her the document, Cindy asked who opposing counsel was. Ms. Nelson identified Lindsay Swiger — Amentum's defense counsel at Jackson Lewis P.C. Cindy indicated she was familiar with her. Cindy then asked if Ms. Nelson knew Marie Mattox, left the room to make a phone call, and returned stating Ms. Nelson needed to go speak with Marie Mattox's office. When Ms. Nelson clarified that all she needed was a document review for shotgun pleadings, Cindy refused and stated she could not assist because the filing deadline was the next day.

Upon arriving at Marie Mattox's office, an attorney informed Ms. Nelson that he had already contacted Lindsay Swiger — without Ms. Nelson's knowledge or authorization — to ask whether Swiger would agree to a filing extension. The conversation then shifted to mediation and the attorney proceeded to act on Ms. Nelson's behalf as though a representation agreement existed — without one ever being signed or discussed. Ms. Nelson had not authorized anyone to contact opposing counsel, discuss her case, act on her behalf, or make decisions about her legal strategy.

Ms. Nelson left without receiving the free help she had driven from South Carolina to obtain. She researched the shotgun pleadings issue herself, corrected the document, and successfully filed her amended complaint the following day with the United States District Court for the Northern District of Florida.

The Significance

The coordinator of a free Veterans legal clinic recognized Amentum's defense counsel by name, redirected Ms. Nelson to a private law firm under false pretenses, and that firm contacted opposing counsel without authorization — then attempted to steer Ms. Nelson toward mediation while acting as though a representation relationship existed. A veteran seeking free legal help should be able to trust that her case details are confidential and that no one in that room is working against her interests. This incident is consistent with the broader pattern of interference with Ms. Nelson's legal proceedings documented throughout this site.

The Pattern

Defense contractors with critical government contracts have historically operated with reduced accountability. The assumption — often correct — is that no institution will hold them accountable because the political and financial cost of doing so is too high.

Ms. Nelson's case documents what happens when that assumption drives institutional behavior — when a defense contractor can discriminate, retaliate, destroy evidence, intercept private communications, and coordinate with government agencies and a Congressional office to silence a whistleblower — and face no consequences.

The House Judiciary Committee has both the authority and the obligation to investigate. Congressional oversight of defense contractor conduct — and of the judicial proceedings that are supposed to hold them accountable — is not optional. It is essential.
