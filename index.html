The Case

What This Is About

Debra Nelson, a former employee of Curtiss-Wright — a major U.S. defense contractor involved in shipbuilding — filed a whistleblower complaint in 2023 after experiencing unlawful discrimination. What followed was a two-year coordinated campaign of retaliation involving cyber intrusion, physical surveillance, evidence destruction, and the failure of every federal institution designed to protect whistleblowers.

This site exists because the courts, the FBI, the Department of Defense Inspector General, the NLRB and a sitting Congressman's office have all failed to act. The public record presented here is the last recourse. Sunlight is the best disinfectant.

The Central Unanswered Question

"How did Congressman Clyburn's office transmit a document referencing Ms. Nelson to the Department of Defense — before she had given that document to Congressman Clyburn's office?"

Claims Dismissed With Prejudice as Sanctions

ECF 316 — District Judge Richard Mark Gergel adopted Magistrate Judge Baker's sanctions recommendation in part after finding willful discovery noncompliance. Rather than dismissing the entire case, the Court imposed lesser sanctions by dismissing four claims with prejudice, striking emotional distress and compensatory damages, and prohibiting use of withheld evidence on remaining claims.

Whistleblower Retaliation41 U.S.C. § 4712

Also ordered: emotional distress damages struck; compensatory damages struck; Plaintiff prohibited from using withheld evidence to support remaining claims. This order contradicts Judge Baker's own ECF 250 Report and Recommendation that these claims should survive.

Plaintiff's Position: Why This Order Will Be Reversed on Appeal The dismissed claims are the same claims Judge Baker's ECF 250 Report and Recommendation stated should survive. The dismissal rests on a defective Jackson Lewis subpoena with three documented procedural violations. The controlling Supreme Court precedent — Jaffee v. Redmond, 518 U.S. 1 (1996) — protects psychotherapist-patient communications from compelled disclosure. These are the primary grounds for Ms. Nelson's pending Fourth Circuit appeal.

Broader Pattern

Defense Contractor Accountability

The misconduct documented in this case is not an isolated incident. It reflects a pattern of behavior by defense contractors who operate under the assumption that their government contracts make them immune from accountability — immune from discrimination laws, immune from whistleblower protections, and immune from judicial scrutiny.

The Core Problem

"If a defense contractor's shipbuilding contracts are critical to national security — then the integrity of the people executing those contracts matters. Discrimination, retaliation, and cover-up inside a defense contractor is not just a labor issue. It is a national security concern."

Curtiss-Wright — Primary Case
Nelson v. Curtiss-Wright — Case No. 2:25-CV-00405-RMG-MGB

Curtiss-Wright Electro Mechanical Corporation — a major U.S. defense contractor involved in shipbuilding — discriminated against Ms. Nelson, retaliated against her whistleblower complaint, and has since engaged in a two-year coordinated campaign of obstruction documented throughout this site.

Deposition testimony obtained during discovery confirms that discrimination at Curtiss-Wright is not historical — it is ongoing. Other employees have experienced the same conduct. Ms. Nelson is the one who fought back.

The Pattern

Defense contractors with critical government contracts have historically operated with reduced accountability. The assumption — often correct — is that no institution will hold them accountable because the political and financial cost of doing so is too high.

Ms. Nelson's case documents what happens when that assumption drives institutional behavior — when a defense contractor can discriminate, retaliate, destroy evidence, intercept private communications, and coordinate with government agencies and a Congressional office to silence a whistleblower — and face no consequences.

The House Judiciary Committee has both the authority and the obligation to investigate. Congressional oversight of defense contractor conduct — and of the judicial proceedings that are supposed to hold them accountable — is not optional. It is essential.
