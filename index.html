The Case

What This Is About

Debra Nelson, a former employee of Curtiss-Wright — a major U.S. defense contractor involved in shipbuilding — filed a whistleblower complaint in 2023 after experiencing unlawful discrimination. What followed was a two-year coordinated campaign of retaliation involving cyber intrusion, physical surveillance, evidence destruction, and the failure of every federal institution designed to protect whistleblowers.

This site exists because the courts, the FBI, the Department of Defense Inspector General, the NLRB and a sitting Congressman's office have all failed to act. The public record presented here is the last recourse. Sunlight is the best disinfectant.

The Central Unanswered Question

"How did Congressman Clyburn's office transmit a document referencing Ms. Nelson to the Department of Defense — before she had given that document to Congressman Clyburn's office?"

Judicial Misconduct

What Happened Inside the Courtroom

The misconduct in this case is not limited to events outside the courtroom. The judicial record itself documents a pattern of rulings that systematically blocked Ms. Nelson's ability to obtain evidence — and a direct contradiction between two judges regarding a court-issued subpoena.

The Central Judicial Misconduct Question

"Magistrate Judge Cherry reviewed the evidence and issued the subpoenas. Magistrate Judge Baker — her replacement — then withdrew those same subpoenas and told Ms. Nelson the court had never issued them. The prior signed order remains on the docket."

Broader Pattern

Defense Contractor Accountability

The misconduct documented in this case is not an isolated incident. It reflects a pattern of behavior by defense contractors who operate under the assumption that their government contracts make them immune from accountability — immune from discrimination laws, immune from whistleblower protections, and immune from judicial scrutiny.

The Core Problem

"If a defense contractor's shipbuilding contracts are critical to national security — then the integrity of the people executing those contracts matters. Discrimination, retaliation, and cover-up inside a defense contractor is not just a labor issue. It is a national security concern."

Curtiss-Wright — Primary Case
Nelson v. Curtiss-Wright — Case No. 2:25-CV-00405-RMG-MGB

Curtiss-Wright Electro Mechanical Corporation — a major U.S. defense contractor involved in shipbuilding — discriminated against Ms. Nelson, retaliated against her whistleblower complaint, and has since engaged in a two-year coordinated campaign of obstruction documented throughout this site.

Deposition testimony obtained during discovery confirms that discrimination at Curtiss-Wright is not historical — it is ongoing. Other employees have experienced the same conduct. Ms. Nelson is the one who fought back.

Pending — Awaiting Ruling

Multiple motions remain pending before the court. The public record documented on this site raises serious questions about whether those rulings will be made impartially. Congressional oversight of both the defense contractor conduct and the judicial proceedings is warranted and urgently needed.

ECF NO. 251 — DATE FILED 12/15/25
Order on Discovery Motions — Psychotherapy Records
Magistrate Judge Mary Gordon Baker
JUDGE BAKER

This order compels Ms. Nelson to produce psychotherapy records or abandon her emotional distress claims. The order contains an internal contradiction — it cites cases supporting the "narrow approach" to psychotherapist-patient privilege waiver, while simultaneously ordering broad disclosure. The Internal Contradiction — ECF 251 at Page 15

The order simultaneously states: (1) Defendants are entitled to "full and fair discovery" of therapy records if Ms. Nelson pursues emotional distress claims AND (2) Plaintiff is the "master and commander of the release of her medical information" and may abandon those claims to protect her privacy. The cases cited in the order — Cappetta, Richardson, Brooks — all support the narrow approach to waiver and do not authorize the broad disclosure demanded.

Defective Defense Subpoena — Three Procedural Violations

Jackson Lewis served a subpoena on Ms. Nelson's therapist that: (1) listed a compliance date of October 31, 2025 but was mailed November 3, 2025 — after its own deadline; (2) was issued without a Court signature in violation of Rule 45; (3) sent Page 1 twice while omitting Page 3 — the page containing the therapist's statutory rights under Rule 45. A facially invalid subpoena was used as the basis for compelling production of Ms. Nelson's most private medical records.

Six-Month Harassment Pattern

Ms. Nelson documented in her May 4, 2026 objections that defendants pursued her private psychotherapy records for over six months — demanding all records without limitation, including irrelevant personal and family sessions. The court did not address this pattern of harassing discovery conduct.

Why This Order Will Be Reversed on Appeal

The claims dismissed here are the exact claims Judge Baker's own ECF 250 Report and Recommendation stated should survive. The basis for dismissal — failure to produce psychotherapy records — relies on a defective Jackson Lewis subpoena with three documented procedural violations. The controlling Supreme Court precedent (Jaffee v. Redmond, 518 U.S. 1, 1996) protects psychotherapist-patient communications. These are the primary grounds for Ms. Nelson's Fourth Circuit appeal.
